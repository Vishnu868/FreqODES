FreqODES: Viva Questions & Answers

22AIE437 Medical Image Processing · Replication of ODES (MICCAI 2025) + frequency-domain image pruning · CHAOS T1-DUAL in-phase → out-of-phase

30-second pitch: ODES adapts a segmentation model to a new MRI domain one patient at a time, asking an expert to label only 1% of pixels on a few selected slices. It picks those slices by comparing batch-norm statistics inside the network, which needs 20 forward passes per slice. Our novelty (FreqODES) picks the slices from the image's frequency content (FFT ring energies + Haar wavelet sub-band energies) instead. It detects the shift almost perfectly (AUC 0.994) and is about 35% faster. Alone it trails ODES by less than 1 Dice point; fused with the original score it matches ODES (79.09 vs 78.83, not significant). The lesson: a good shift detector is not automatically a good annotation selector.

1. Problem & motivation

Q1What problem does your project solve?

Segmentation models trained on one MRI scanner or sequence (source) perform worse on another (target) because the image appearance changes. This is domain shift. We adapt the model to the target domain online, patient by patient, using a very small amount of expert labelling.

Q2Why is domain shift important clinically?

Hospitals use different scanners, protocols and sequences. A model that works in one hospital can silently fail in another, giving wrong organ boundaries for surgery planning or volume measurement. In our data, the model drops from ~79% Dice (after adaptation) to 55% without adaptation, and kidney Dice falls to about 30%.

Q3Why not just retrain the model on the new data?

Retraining needs fully labelled target data. Expert labelling of 3-D MRI is slow and expensive. ODES needs labels for only 1% of pixels on a subset of slices, and it updates only batch-norm layers, so adaptation is quick and cheap.

Q4Why not use fully unsupervised test-time adaptation like TENT?

Unsupervised methods rely on the model's own pseudo-labels, which can be wrong under shift and are risky in clinical use. In the paper, TENT reaches 67.3 Dice on CHAOS IP→OOP, while ODES with 1% expert labels reaches 78.6.

2. Dataset & setup

Q5Which dataset did you use?

CHAOS (Combined Healthy Abdominal Organ Segmentation) challenge, MR T1-DUAL sequence: 20 patients, 647 axial slices (26–50 per patient), resized to 256×256. Labels: liver, right kidney, left kidney, spleen + background (5 classes).

Q6What are in-phase and out-of-phase images? Why is that a domain shift?

T1-DUAL is a gradient-echo sequence acquired at two echo times. In in-phase (IP) images, fat and water signals add up. In out-of-phase (OOP) images, they cancel at fat–water boundaries, producing dark "India-ink" edges and different texture. The anatomy is identical but the appearance differs, which makes it a clean domain-shift benchmark.

Q7What is your train / test split?

Following the paper: the source model is trained on in-phase volumes. The 20 out-of-phase volumes arrive as an online stream, one patient = one batch, processed once. Each patient is evaluated right after its own adaptation step. There is no separate target training set, because the setting is online.

If asked about leakage: the same patients appear in source and target, as in the paper's protocol. This tests appearance shift, not patient generalisation.

Q8Which metrics did you use?

Dice (2|A∩B| / (|A|+|B|)) and Jaccard / IoU (|A∩B| / |A∪B|) per organ and averaged, adaptation time, and a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the 20 patients to check whether differences are significant.

3. Base paper (ODES)

Q9What is the base paper?

"ODES: Online Domain Adaptation with Expert Guidance for Medical Image Segmentation", Islam et al., University of California Riverside, MICCAI 2025. Code: github.com/ShazidAraf/ODES.

Q10Explain the ODES pipeline step by step.

For each incoming patient:

1. Infer: the source-trained DeepLabv3 predicts every slice.
2. Image pruning: keep the top-K% slices with the largest domain shift.
3. Acquisition: on those slices, choose 5×5 patches covering b = 1% of pixels, where the model is most uncertain.
4. Expert labels those patches.
5. Update only the batch-norm γ and β with cross-entropy on the labelled pixels, then move on to the next patient.

Q11What is the model architecture?

DeepLabv3 with a ResNet-18 encoder (ImageNet-initialised, via timm), an ASPP module (atrous spatial pyramid pooling, multi-scale context), a low-level skip connection from an early layer, and a 5-class head. Output is upsampled to 256×256.

Q12How does ODES measure domain shift (its image-pruning score)?

Each slice is augmented 20 times (flips, rotations) and passed through a copy of the network. The batch-norm layers record the mean and variance of the activations. Treating each channel as a Gaussian, ODES computes the KL divergence between the source running statistics and these new statistics (last 9 BN layers). A higher KL means a bigger shift.

Q13What is the acquisition function?

A = U ⊙ P ⊙ W, where U is patch-mean entropy (uncertainty), P is regional impurity (how many classes are mixed in the window), and W is a diversity map built with farthest-first traversal, so selected patches are spread out spatially and in feature space. Patches are taken greedily at the maxima of A.

Q14Why update only batch-norm layers?

BN layers hold the feature statistics that change most under domain shift. Updating only γ and β is a tiny number of parameters, so it is fast, stable with very few labels, and avoids catastrophic forgetting.

Q16Why are your numbers slightly different from the paper?

The paper releases no source checkpoint and no training recipe, so we trained our own source model. Kidney scores differ most for this reason. GPU randomness also causes small run-to-run differences (we saw about ±0.5 Dice between two runs).

Q17What did you have to fix or add in the public code?

1. A CHAOS preprocessing script (DICOM/PNG → stacks), with a check that slices align with their labels.
2. A source-training script (missing upstream).
3. FFT_weight_2.py: imported by the code but never released. We re-implemented it from the paper's Algorithm 2.

5. Fixed a bug where the previous slice's acquisition map could be used.
6. Made it run on CPU/GPU (CUDA was hard-coded), and added Jaccard and JSON outputs.

Q18What were your training settings?

PyTorch; Adam, lr 1e-3, weight decay 1e-4, cosine schedule; batch size 8; 25 epochs; loss = cross-entropy + Dice; augmentation = flips, 90° rotations, intensity jitter. Adaptation: Adam, lr 1e-2, one step per patient, BN only. Hardware: NVIDIA Tesla T4 (Google Colab). Seed 1337. Training loss went from 1.07 to 0.16.

5. Novelty: FreqODES

Q19What exactly is your novelty?

We replaced ODES's network-based slice-selection score with a frequency-domain score computed directly from the image:

FFT descriptor: energy in 16 concentric frequency rings of the 2-D Fourier spectrum (low → high frequency).
DWT descriptor: energy of LL, LH, HL, HH sub-bands of a 3-level Haar wavelet transform.
We compare each slice's descriptor with the average of 200 source slices (z-score distance). The slices with the largest distance are sent to the expert.
Fused version: D = λ·BN-score + (1−λ)·frequency-score.

Everything else in ODES is unchanged, so any difference comes only from the selection score.

Q20Why did you expect frequency features to work?

Scanner and sequence changes alter texture, noise and edge content, which show up directly as changes in spatial-frequency energy. OOP images have chemical-shift artefacts at fat–water boundaries, which add high-frequency energy. Our analysis confirms this: OOP slices have clearly more high-frequency energy in every band.

Q21Write the formula for your shift score.

Dfreq(x) = (1/d) Σf ((φf(x) − μf) / σf)²

φf is the f-th descriptor value (log relative energy of a band or sub-band), and μf, σf are the mean and standard deviation over 200 source slices. It is a diagonal Mahalanobis distance.

Q22Why use log relative energy, not raw energy?

Relative energy (each band divided by the total) removes overall brightness and contrast differences, so the score measures the shape of the spectrum, not intensity. Log scale makes the small high-frequency bands comparable to the large low-frequency band.

Q23Why both FFT and wavelets?

FFT gives global frequency content but no location. Wavelets are localised in both space and frequency, and separate horizontal, vertical and diagonal detail. Using both gives complementary views. Both are Unit 2 topics in the MIP syllabus (domain transformation).

Q24How do you know the frequency score actually detects the shift?

We used the score alone to separate held-out in-phase slices from out-of-phase slices: ROC-AUC 0.994 (FFT), 0.969 (DWT), 0.993 (both). Its mean score is 2.6 for IP vs 21.3 for OOP. It also rises steadily when we add synthetic noise or blur to source images.

Q26So did your modification improve the results?

Not in accuracy. Alone, the frequency score is 0.6–0.8 Dice lower than ODES at K = 10% (significant for FFT, p = 0.005). Fused with λ = 0.25 it is slightly higher (79.09 vs 78.83), but not significant (p = 0.75), so we say it matches ODES.

Yes in speed and interpretability: about 35% faster (no forward passes for selection), and every choice can be explained with a spectrum plot.

Q27Why did the frequency score not beat ODES?

1. Objective mismatch: it measures "how different is this slice?", not "which labels help the model most?". The most frequency-shifted slices are often at the top and bottom of the volume, with tiny or no organs, so labelling them teaches little. The BN score reflects what the network finds unusual, which links more closely to its errors.
2. Global shift: IP→OOP affects every slice, so almost all slices look "shifted". This is also why random selection is competitive.
3. Saturation: all methods land between 78 and 80 Dice, close to labelling every slice, so the selection criterion has little room to matter.
4. Small effects: differences under 1 point, from one seed and 20 patients.

Q28Random selection does almost as well. Doesn't that make the pruning step pointless?

On this pair, yes, the pruning step adds little: random (78.75) vs ODES (78.83), p = 0.33. This differs from the paper's Table 3. Because the IP→OOP shift is uniform across slices, there is little to rank. Pruning should matter more where shift varies between slices or patients, e.g. CHAOS → DUKE (different scanners), which is our suggested future work.

Q29What did the ablation studies show?

Diversity weighting on vs off (K = 10%): BN 78.73 → 78.83, FFT+DWT 78.32 → 78.20, random 78.96 → 78.75. Changes are ≤ 0.2 points, much smaller than the paper reports.
Fusion weight λ: 0 → 78.20, 0.25 → 79.09, 0.5 → 78.40, 0.75 → 78.93, 1 (pure ODES) → 78.83. Adding any BN information brings accuracy back to the ODES level.

Q30Why use the Wilcoxon test?

Each method is evaluated on the same 20 patients, so results are paired. Wilcoxon signed-rank is a non-parametric paired test, so it doesn't assume per-patient differences are normally distributed, which suits only 20 samples.

Q31Is the comparison fair?

Yes. Same data, same source model, same seed (1337), same patient order, same budgets (b = 1%, same K) and same acquisition and update steps. Only the slice-selection score changes.

7. Implementation

Q32Walk me through the code structure.

prepare_chaos.py converts the data · train_source.py trains the source model · demo_ODES.py runs the online loop and evaluation · methods/ODES.py holds pruning, acquisition and the BN update · methods/freq_shift.py is our FFT/DWT score · run_all.sh runs all 19 experiments · make_report.py builds tables, figures and the LaTeX numbers · colab_run.sh runs the whole pipeline end to end.

Q33How do you switch between ODES and FreqODES?

One config option: ACTIVE.PRUNE = BN (ODES), FFT, DWT, FREQ (FFT+DWT), FUSED (with ACTIVE.FUSE_LAMBDA) or RANDOM. Example:
python demo_ODES.py --cfg cfgs/CHAOS_T1_IP_OOP.yaml ACTIVE.PRUNE FREQ ACTIVE.BUDGET_SLICE 0.1

Q34How long does it take to run?

On a T4: source training about 10–15 min; each online pass over 20 patients 107–173 s; the full 19-run sweep about 45–60 min. Downloading CHAOS from Zenodo was the slowest part.

Q35How do you make sure the numbers in the slides and report are correct?

make_report.py reads the result JSON files and writes every number into numbers.tex as LaTeX macros. The slides and report use these macros, so no number is typed by hand.

8. Limitations & future work

Q36What are the limitations?

One seed, 20 patients, one adaptation pair (IP→OOP). Our source model is not identical to the paper's. Expert labels are simulated from ground truth. Differences between methods are under 1 Dice point.

Q37What would you try next?

1. Weight the frequency score by predicted organ area or uncertainty, so empty end-slices are not picked.
2. Compute spectra only inside the organ region.
3. Test CHAOS → DUKE and BMC → RUNMC, where shift is larger and varies more.
4. Several seeds and patient orders.
5. Learn λ automatically.

Q38What is the main takeaway of your project?

We reproduced ODES and showed that an image-based FFT/wavelet score detects MRI domain shift almost perfectly and much faster. But detecting shift is not the same as choosing the most useful slices to label. The best accuracy comes from combining image-level frequency cues with model-level information.

9. Concept basics (quick recall)

Q39What is the 2-D Fourier transform of an image?

It represents the image as a sum of sinusoidal patterns of different frequencies and orientations. The centre of the shifted spectrum is low frequency (smooth regions); the outer parts are high frequency (edges, fine texture, noise).

Q40What is a Haar wavelet transform?

It repeatedly splits an image into a low-pass approximation (LL) and three detail sub-bands: LH (horizontal edges), HL (vertical edges) and HH (diagonal detail). Each level halves the resolution. Unlike the FFT, it keeps spatial location.

Q41What is batch normalisation?

It normalises each channel's activations using a mean and variance (running averages at test time), then scales and shifts them with learned γ and β. Under domain shift the stored source statistics no longer match the target, which is why ODES adapts BN layers.

Q42What is active learning?

The model chooses which samples (here, pixels or patches) an expert should label, to get the most improvement from the fewest labels, usually based on uncertainty and diversity.

Q43Dice vs Jaccard: what is the difference?

Dice = 2|A∩B| / (|A|+|B|); Jaccard = |A∩B| / |A∪B|. They are monotonically related: J = D / (2 − D). Jaccard is always lower and penalises errors more.
